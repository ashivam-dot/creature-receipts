# ep026 source and visual receipt — Peshtigo Fire

Checked directly on 2026-10-03. Claim numbers match research.json and the citations in script.json. The script uses the [Wisconsin State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/wisconsin-peshtigo-fire/) for the weather, fire duration, destruction, and death estimate; the [Peshtigo Fire Museum](https://peshtigofiremuseum.com/fire/chapter-3/) for Father Pernin's eyewitness account; and a [Green Bay Press-Gazette retrospective republished by the National Weather Service](https://www.weather.gov/grb/peshtigofire) for Chicago's lasting attention. The last source is identified by its actual authorship rather than treated as NWS climate research.

## Spoken beat audit

| Beat | Spoken text | Claims | Direct receipt |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Up to twenty-five hundred people died in the Peshtigo Fire. | 1 | [Wisconsin State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/wisconsin-peshtigo-fire/): “Between 1,200 and 2,500 people lost their lives.” “Up to” expresses the upper end of an estimate. |
| 2 | October eighth, eighteen seventy-one: Wisconsin's forests were dry. | 2 | [Wisconsin State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/wisconsin-peshtigo-fire/): “On October 8 and 9, 1871” the village was demolished; warm and dry conditions “extended into the fall season”; low humidity “reduced the moisture content of dead logs, branches, and brush on the forest floor.” |
| 3 | Strong southwest winds fanned smoldering fires across Wisconsin. | 3 | [Wisconsin State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/wisconsin-peshtigo-fire/): “strong southwest winds to blow over Wisconsin and Michigan”; “By evening, strong winds fanned the smoldering fires.” No speed is asserted. |
| 4 | A firestorm swallowed Peshtigo in less than two hours. | 4 | [Wisconsin State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/wisconsin-peshtigo-fire/): “As the flames grew, a firestorm was created”; “Peshtigo was consumed in less than two hours by the wall of fire.” No fire temperature is asserted. |
| 5 | Father Peter Pernin survived by sheltering in the river. | 5 | [Peshtigo Fire Museum](https://peshtigofiremuseum.com/fire/chapter-3/), “After the Fire,” quotes Pernin: “after having been so long in the river” and recounts his recovery and later duties. The new line omits the unsourced word “freezing.” |
| 6 | Only two buildings remained. It became America's deadliest wildfire. | 6, 7 | [Wisconsin State Climatology Office](https://climatology.nelson.wisc.edu/wisconsin-peshtigo-fire/): “When the smoke had cleared, only two buildings remained”; “the deadliest wildfire in America’s history occurred in northeastern Wisconsin.” |
| 7 | Chicago burned that same night and took the headlines. | 8 | [Peshtigo Fire Museum](https://peshtigofiremuseum.com/fire/chapter-3/): “the Chicago Fire (which burned the same night as the Peshtigo Fire).” The [Press-Gazette story hosted by NWS](https://www.weather.gov/grb/peshtigofire) says the Peshtigo anniversary “usually receives little note outside the region because another horrific fire the same night -- the great Chicago Fire -- still seems to hog the headlines.” |

The original ledger's 110 mph wind and 2,000°F fire temperature were supported only by weaker secondary material; the Wisconsin source gives neither measurement. The script and cards now use the directly sourced weather and duration instead. The former “400 miles south” and “freezing river” details were also removed. The older title's “forgotten” implication was narrowed to the documented difference in public attention.

The Library of Congress catalog links embedded in Commons metadata and the Wisconsin article returned HTTP 403 from this environment. Their individual catalog records were therefore not independently opened; date, artist, and rights checks for those images rest on Commons imageinfo metadata.
