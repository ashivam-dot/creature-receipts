# ep047 research: How Did the Alamo Fall in About 90 Minutes? (The Last Hours)

Originally researched 2026-10-03 by the cloud studio (gemini-3.6-flash, gemini-3.7-flash). The editorial correction below also uses directly checked Texas State Historical Association and Texas State Library accounts; the revised duration bounds each cite one institutional source.

Story (corrected 2026-10-03): During the Alamo’s 13-day siege, Travis asked for aid. Thirty-two men from Gonzales entered on March 1, but the fort still needed far more support. Around dawn on March 6, about 1,800 Mexican attackers advanced on the damaged walls. The final assault lasted no more than 90 minutes. On April 21, the Texian army defeated Santa Anna at San Jacinto in less than 20 minutes.

## Disputed or left out

- The story of Travis drawing a line in the sand is considered legend and lacks primary source evidence; a script should attribute this to tradition or leave it out.
- Accounts of David Crockett's death vary between him dying in combat or being among a small group executed after surrendering; a script should state that historians disagree.
- The exact number of Texian defenders varies between 182 and 257; a script should use 'nearly 200'.
- Specific details of Jim Bowie's death are conflicting, with some saying he died fighting from his bed and others saying he was bayoneted while unconscious; a script should attribute these as 'conflicting accounts'.

## Editorial correction receipt (2026-10-03)

The held episode remains under `editorial_hold.json`; no release is implied by this draft. The original beat 3 said reinforcements “never came,” contradicting the saved claim that 32 men from Gonzales entered on March 1. The Texas State Historical Association confirms their arrival and says Travis still needed more support. The same account says the final assault lasted **no more than 90 minutes**, after the 13-day siege. Texas State Library quotes Travis's March 3 blood-red banner letter and describes San Jacinto as lasting **less than 20 minutes**; the replacement uses those bounds.

The February 24 Travis letter is labeled as an appeal, and the assault beat now uses Robert Jenkins Onderdonk's circa 1903 painting rather than the letter. The later Travis portrait, 1854 Alamo ruins painting, and 1898 San Jacinto painting also carry visible dates/provenance labels.
