# Content calendar

The studio takes topics from here: anniversaries dated within the next 7 days first, then the backlog,
rotating through the series and taking each one's most-read topic (by English Wikipedia pageviews) first.
It marks a topic `— making (epNNN)` when its Short starts and `— done (epNNN)` when the Short is
scheduled. A Short that fails the quality gate is `— parked` (it comes back once after 14 days), and one
that fails again, or whose research is too thin, is `— dropped`. The daily routine adds new topics on its
own (`auto.add_topics`).

Each topic is one line naming the story, its year if it has one, and its twist, with an English Wikipedia
article behind it. Every topic still has to pass the two-source rule.

Seeded 2026-09-30. Each line starts with the exact title of its English Wikipedia article (checked with the
API: it exists, isn't a redirect or a disambiguation page), and each twist was checked against that article's
text. Space topics belong to the sister channel Universe Receipts and never go here.

## Backlog

### Built Different

- Axolotl: it can regrow limbs and even parts of its heart and brain, yet only about 50 to 1,000 adults are left in the wild — done (ep009)
- Tardigrade (1773): named "little water bear" in 1773, a dried-out one can survive from −272 °C to 149 °C, at least briefly — parked (ep015)
- Octopus: three hearts, blue copper-based blood, and two-thirds of its 500 million neurons in its arms — making (ep022)
- Wombat: it leaves cube-shaped droppings, and the shape keeps them from rolling off the spots it marks
- Mantis shrimp: its club strikes at 23 meters a second, so fast that collapsing cavitation bubbles hit the prey a second time
- Cuttlefish: it can't see color, yet it changes the color of its skin to blend in
- Electric eel (1775): it isn't an eel, it delivers up to 860 volts, and it turned out to be three species in 2019
- Naked mole-rat: it survives 18 minutes with no oxygen at all and lives past 37 years, longer than any other rodent
- Bombardier beetle: it fires a spray heated to near 100 °C in about 70 pulses at 500 a second
- Hagfish: the only living animal with a skull but no backbone, it escapes a predator by tying itself in a knot
- Lyrebird: lyrebirds have been recorded copying chainsaws, car alarms, camera shutters, and crying babies
- Monarch butterfly migration: monarchs fly up to 3,000 miles to Mexico, and the ones heading north are at least four generations removed
- Alpheidae: the snapping shrimp's claw fires a bubble so loud that shrimp colonies interfere with navy sonar
- Wood frog: in Alaska it spends about 193 days frozen, with no heartbeat, breathing, or brain activity
- Archerfish: it shoots insects off leaves with a jet of water, correcting for the way light bends at the surface
- Common basilisk: the "Jesus Christ lizard" gets its name from running across the surface of water

### Deep Sea Files

- Giant squid (2004): nobody photographed a live one in its habitat until September 30, 2004, and its eyes reach at least 27 cm across — dropped (ep019)
- Colossal squid (2025): it has the largest eyes of any animal known to have existed, and was first filmed alive in its habitat on March 9, 2025 — making (ep012)
- Anglerfish: the tiny male latches onto a far larger female and can fuse into her body — making (ep023)
- Goblin shark: its jaws launch forward out of its head, from a lineage about 125 million years old
- Cookiecutter shark: in the 1980s about 30 US Navy submarines were damaged by its bites
- Vampire squid (1903): first described as an octopus, it is neither squid nor octopus, and it eats drifting "marine snow", not blood
- Giant oarfish: the world's longest bony fish, up to about 8 meters and possibly 11, is the likely source of many sea serpent sightings
- Giant isopod: a captive one at Japan's Toba Aquarium refused food for 5 years
- Grimpoteuthis: the dumbo octopus, named for its ear-like fins, is the deepest-living octopus known
- Scaly-foot gastropod: the only known animal that builds iron sulfide into its skeleton, and in 2019 the first species listed as endangered because of deep-sea mining
- Frilled shark: a pregnancy can last up to 3.5 years, and it has 300 needle-like teeth
- Macropinna (2008): a fish with a see-through head, whose tube-shaped eyes turned out in 2008 to rotate inside it
- Riftia: the giant tube worm has no mouth or gut, lives on bacteria inside its body, and grows faster than any other known marine invertebrate
- Pseudoliparis swirei: the Mariana snailfish lives more than 8,000 meters down in the Mariana Trench
- Kiwa hirsuta (2005): the "yeti crab" found in 2005 at hydrothermal vents 2,200 meters deep grows bacteria on its hairy claws

### Back From Extinction

- Coelacanth (1938): a fish thought extinct for 66 million years turned up in a South African fisherman's catch on December 22, 1938 — done (ep011)
- Pyrenean ibex (2003): the first animal ever brought back from extinction by cloning lived only minutes
- Przewalski's horse (2020): extinct in the wild after 1969, it now has a clone named Kurt, born in 2020 from a long-dead stallion's DNA
- Black-footed ferret (1981): declared extinct in 1979, found in Wyoming in 1981, and every ferret alive today descends from just 7 animals
- Dire wolf (2025): the "dire wolf" pups announced in 2025 were gray wolves with 20 edits to 14 genes — done (ep018)
- Dryococelus (2001): the "tree lobster" was wiped out by rats from a 1918 shipwreck, then found in 2001 as 24 insects on a sea stack called Ball's Pyramid
- Megachile pluto (1981): the world's largest bee, with a 63.5 mm wingspan, was lost to science until 1981 and first filmed alive in 2019
- Fernandina Island Galápagos tortoise (2019): known from one specimen collected in 1906, until a single female was found alive in 2019
- Laotian rock rat (2005): found for sale as meat in a Laos market, then linked to a family thought extinct for 11 million years
- Zaglossus attenboroughi (2023): Attenborough's echidna was unseen by scientists from 1961 until a trail camera caught one in 2023
- Mountain pygmy possum (1966): known only from fossils since 1895, then found alive at a ski resort on Mount Hotham in 1966
- Bermuda petrel (1951): thought extinct for 300 years, then 18 nesting pairs were found in 1951, and a 15-year-old boy there became its chief conservationist
- Chacoan peccary (1930): described from fossils in 1930 as an extinct species, then found alive in 1971
- Cuban solenodon (1974): a venomous mammal declared extinct in 1970 and found again in 1974
- De Winton's golden mole (2023): unseen for 86 years, then tracked down in 2023 with environmental DNA and sniffer dogs
- La Gomera giant lizard (1999): described from ancient remains in 1985, then six living lizards were found in 1999

### Evolution Got Weird

- Platypus (1799): the first scientists to examine one thought it was a fake sewn together from several animals, and it glows under ultraviolet light — parked (ep016)
- Horseshoe crab: it isn't a crab but a closer relative of spiders, and its blue blood is used to test for bacterial toxins
- Carcinisation (1916): evolution has turned non-crabs into crab shapes at least five separate times
- Narwhal: the "horn" is a spiral left canine tooth up to 3 meters long, packed with millions of nerve endings- Kiwi (bird): its egg can weigh 20% of the mother, and its closest relative is the extinct elephant bird of Madagascar, not the moa — making (ep021)
- Tuatara (2009): it isn't a lizard but the last of an ancient order, and a male named Henry became a father at 111
- Sloth: its fur grows algae that feed moths found nowhere else, and it climbs down about once a week to use the bathroom
- Clownfish: when the group's female disappears, the breeding male changes sex and takes her place
- Seahorse: the male carries the eggs, up to 1,500 of them, in a brood pouch
- Aye-aye: a lemur that taps on wood with a thin middle finger to find grubs, filling the niche of a woodpecker
- Hoatzin: its chicks have claws on their wings, and it ferments leaves in its gut the way cattle do
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve: in a giraffe this nerve takes a detour of about 4.6 meters, a leftover from fish ancestors
- Evolution of cetaceans: whales descend from hoofed land mammals, and their closest living relatives are hippos
- Glass frog (2022): it hides most of its blood in its liver while it sleeps, which makes it far harder to see
- Common cuckoo: it lays its eggs in other birds' nests, and the female mimics a sparrowhawk, which buys her time to lay
- Elysia chlorotica: a sea slug that steals chloroplasts from algae and keeps them working for months
- Bdelloidea (2021): no male has ever been seen, and in 2021 some were revived after 24,000 years in Siberian permafrost
- Mexican tetra: cave populations of this fish have lost their eyes completely

### Nature's Record Breakers

- Greenland shark: the longest-lived vertebrate known, at least 272 years, and it doesn't mature until about 150
- Blue whale: the largest animal ever known, with a heart that weighed 180 kg
- Sperm whale: the loudest animal on Earth, at 236 decibels, with the largest brain of any animal — making (ep006)
- Peregrine falcon (2005): a falcon was recorded diving at 389 km/h, though radar has never confirmed speeds like that
- Jonathan (tortoise) (1832): the oldest known living land animal, and an 1886 photo shows him on Saint Helena
- Lion's mane jellyfish (1865): its longest known tentacles, 36.6 meters, were longer than a blue whale
- Japanese spider crab: the largest leg span of any arthropod, up to 3.7 meters claw to claw
- Goliath birdeater: the heaviest spider in the world, at 175 grams
- Bowhead whale: one male was estimated to be 211 years old
- Bee hummingbird: the smallest bird alive, with males weighing about 1.95 grams
- Wisdom (albatross) (1956): banded in 1956 when already about 5, she was still sitting on eggs in 2025
- Arctic tern: one tern flew 96,000 km in ten months, and a lifetime of migrations adds up to about 2.4 million km
- Cuvier's beaked whale: the deepest and longest dives of any mammal, 2,992 meters and 222 minutes
- Common swift: some swifts stay in the air for 10 months straight, eating and sleeping on the wing
- Snowy albatross: the longest wingspan of any living bird, up to 3.7 meters, and it can glide for hours without flapping
- Bar-tailed godwit (2022): a young tagged godwit flew at least 13,560 km nonstop from Alaska to Tasmania in 11 days
- Rüppell's vulture (1973): the highest-flying bird, confirmed at 11,300 meters when one hit a jetliner's engine
- Ming (clam) (2006): a clam dredged off Iceland turned out to be 507 years old — making (ep013)
- Emperor penguin (1994): one penguin dived to 564 meters on a dive lasting 21.8 minutes
- Leatherback sea turtle: the largest living turtle dives as deep as 1,280 meters

### Animal Myths, Busted

- Great white shark: the "Jaws" man-eater normally doesn't hunt people, and most bites come from curiosity or mistaken identity — making (ep017)
- Bat: no species of bat is truly blind
- Camel: the hump stores fat, not water
- Opiliones: the "daddy longlegs is the most venomous animal" story is an urban legend; harvestmen have no venom glands at all
- Porcupine: porcupines can't launch their quills at anything
- Chameleon: chameleons change color mostly to signal each other and react to temperature, not to hide
- Bumblebee (1934): the claim that science says bumblebees can't fly has its earliest known source in a 1934 French book
- Piranha (1913): the feeding frenzy Theodore Roosevelt saw in 1913 was set up by fishermen who had starved a penned school for days
- Goldfish: their memory lasts at least three months, not three seconds
- Common ostrich: ostriches don't bury their heads in the sand, a myth that likely goes back to Pliny the Elder
- Mayfly: the "one-day" insect can spend several years living underwater before it grows wings
- Virginia opossum: the claim that one eats 5,000 ticks a season is a misconception, but it really is naturally resistant to rabies
- Hippopotamus: hippo "blood sweat" is neither blood nor sweat but a natural red sunscreen- Elephants' graveyard: there's no place where old elephants go to die; it's a legend
- Shark cartilage (1992): sharks do get cancer, and the myth that they don't was spread by a 1992 book
