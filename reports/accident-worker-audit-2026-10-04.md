## ep056 — Victoria Hall, Sunderland, 16 June 1883

**Potential first accident pilot after revision and separate approval.** The central sequence is supported by the directly read [University of Sunderland account](https://wp.sunderland.ac.uk/seagullcity/victoria-hall-disaster/) and [BBC account](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cmjvzyxvp0po): gifts were announced; children from the gallery moved down the stairs; a partly bolted door at the bottom opened inward; 183 children died. Use “crowd crush” and make the exit design clear without making the children responsible for the deaths. The [UK National Archives inquest catalogue](https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C4739122) identifies the surviving primary record, but the inquest itself was **not read**. A [Sunderland Council fact sheet](https://www.sunderland.gov.uk/media/6964/5-Victoria-Hall-Disaster/pdf/5_Victoria_Hall_disaster.pdf) was found but returned HTTP 403 from this environment; it was **not inspected**.

The current script's opening and payoff claim that emergency push-bar doors were created by laws forced by this disaster. The worker's research cites Wikipedia and a 2013 [Smithsonian article](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/183-children-died-in-a-stampede-for-toys-in-1883-173637/) for this combined claim. The BBC describes later outward-opening-door requirements and push bars; the university confirms ensuing safety legislation, but neither is the original statute. The [Briggs panic-bolt history](https://www.arthurlloyd.co.uk/RobertBriggsPanicBolts.htm), directly read, cites a separate patent applied for in November 1891 and accepted in August 1892 (`GB18871/1891`). The script conflates legal change, invention and worldwide adoption. Replace its push-bar thesis with the directly supported door-and-crush sequence unless the exact law and patent link are verified in primary records. “Over eleven hundred children packed Victoria Hall” also blurs the roughly 1,100 in the gallery with a larger total audience.

Both saved ep056 reviews have `passed: false` despite high numeric scores. They identify a peaceful modern Mowbray Park image at the narration about children dying as an unsuitable match. The latest spec still uses another modern park-tree photo at that beat. An archival hall plan and a contemporary engraving are listed, but image provenance, depicted event and reuse terms still require item-level inspection. There is no approved final video in this audit.

## ep057 — Saint-Michel-de-Maurienne, 12 December 1917

**Quarantine before any retry; not the first pilot.** The exact calendar hook says a commander forced the driver **at gunpoint** to overload a train with 1,000 soldiers and that it “lost its brakes.” The worker's own `research.json` explicitly says the gunpoint threat is unsupported by its sources and directs the script to omit it. Targeted English and French searches also found no contemporary or official record of a weapon threat. This is **unverified**, not proven false; do not narrate it without a directly inspected primary account. No primary inquiry or court-martial record appears in the saved source list. That list relies on Wikipedia, Grokipedia, a Wikipedia clone, RailTarget, Qualla, France 3 and a municipal commemoration page. Multiple citations recycle the same encyclopedia account and do not provide independent corroboration.

The saved narration omits “gunpoint” but introduces other unsafe claims: “secret military blunder,” “many believe a simple mechanical failure,” “most officers actually abandoned the train for a faster express,” “one engine to pull nineteen coaches,” “the overloaded coaches lost their brakes,” and “the army then hid this tragic failure for ninety years.” The research does not cite evidence for a public belief in simple failure; its numbered claims for the one-engine decision do not establish **nineteen coaches**. “Lost their brakes” compresses a complex braking-capacity failure and should be replaced with sourced, precise wording. The directly read [Saint-Michel-de-Maurienne municipal centenary page](https://www.saint-michel-de-maurienne.com/articles/commemorations) says the disaster remained classified for “many years,” not ninety, and gives 428 dead. The directly read [France 3 retrospective](https://france3-regions.francetvinfo.fr/bourgogne-franche-comte/histoires-14-18-catastrophe-ferroviaire-saint-michel-maurienne-1379367.html) calls it classified for a long time and gives **425** in one passage but **over 675** later. The saved research asserts that an “official declassified report” gives 435, but lists no report. Avoid a precise toll and “90-year cover-up” until the underlying records are reconciled. A public contemporary newspaper notice and a 1923 monument, both noted in the saved research, also require much narrower wording than “the army hid it for ninety years.”

The ep057 review has `passed: false`, scoring visuals 2/5. The current spec uses a **1930** military-exercise photo to depict officers on a **1917** train and reuses it for the derailment; the saved best spec instead includes a fifteenth-century “Image of Death” print at a crash beat. The purported wreck image's Commons filename says **December 1907**, requiring provenance verification before any use. These images cannot stand in for event footage. The draft's visual failures alone block release.
