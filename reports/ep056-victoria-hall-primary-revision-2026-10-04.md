## Legislation and the later panic bolt

- [Hansard, 19 June 1883](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1883/jun/19/public-buildings-doors-bill) records the introduction of the **Public Buildings (Doors) Bill** to require outward-opening doors.
- [Hansard, 28 June 1883, vol. 280 cc1832–34](https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1883/jun/28/bill-239-second-reading) records Bill 239's second-reading debate. Harcourt opposed the bill as inadequate; after discussion, the motion **and bill were withdrawn**. One member explicitly referred to the Sunderland disaster. This directly rules out presenting *that bill* as a law passed after the disaster. It does not rule out later legislation or local regulations.
- The [Briggs panic-bolt history](https://www.arthurlloyd.co.uk/RobertBriggsPanicBolts.htm) reproduces the patent description and identifies application **2 November 1891**, acceptance **13 August 1892**, and reference **GB18871/1891**. This is a researched secondary transcription; the original patent file was **not retrieved**. Its dates and mechanism are separate from the 1883 event and from any building law. No inspected record proves that this accident directly caused Briggs's design, or that every modern push-bar door descends from it.
- [Historic England's listed memorial record](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1207911) and the University of Sunderland broadly connect the disaster to later outward-opening-exit requirements. Sunderland Council's fact sheet returned HTTP 403 here and was not directly inspected. The **specific enacted Act, section, jurisdiction, effective date, and causal chain remain unverified** in primary statutory text. Do not state that Parliament immediately passed a law or that “new laws created push-bar emergency exits.”

## Script-safe revision direction

Retire the current push-bar origin title, opening, legal payoff, and modern emergency-hardware visuals. A candidate sequence for editorial rewriting is:

> **The door that trapped 183 children**. On 16 June 1883, children left the gallery of Sunderland's Victoria Hall after prizes were offered. At the lower stair landing, a floor bolt held a swing door to a narrow opening. The route blocked; the crowd pressed from behind. **183 children died.** Parliament debated outward-opening public doors within days, but its first bill was withdrawn.

This text uses the Annual Register and Hansard without pretending to know the unseen inquest or a later statute. It should still receive a full independent review before any production retry. “Crowd crush” is the preferred label in metadata; avoid “stampede” and wording that assigns fault to the children. The current death beat's modern Mowbray Park trees cannot illustrate the 1883 stairwell or the children who died.
